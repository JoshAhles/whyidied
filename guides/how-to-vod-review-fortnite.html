How to VOD review in Fortnite

UPDATED AUGUST 2026 · 7 MIN READ

VOD review means rewatching your own gameplay to find the decision errors that caused your deaths — and it's the single highest-leverage improvement habit in Fortnite. Aim training makes your crosshair better; VOD review makes your decisions better, and below pro level, decisions cause far more deaths than aim. Here's the method coaches actually use, cut down to something a solo player can run.

What should I record?

Everything, passively. Use a replay buffer — NVIDIA's Shadowplay/instant replay, OBS's replay buffer, or AMD ReLive — so your PC always holds the last several minutes without you thinking about it. The only clips that matter for review are the ~60 seconds before each death, so a buffer beats recording full sessions: when you die, save the buffer, keep playing.

Fortnite also writes a replay file for every match (Career → Replays), which is useful for seeing the map view of your rotations — but replays don't show your actual screen, crosshair, or inputs, so video is the primary source.

Review deaths, not highlights

The core discipline: only review deaths, and only the last 60 seconds before each one. Watching full VODs feels productive and teaches you almost nothing per minute spent. Your deaths are a pre-filtered list of exactly the moments where your decisions failed. Everything you need to learn is concentrated there.

The death-first checklist

For each death, watch the clip twice and answer these questions in order:

1. What was the last mistake? (the peek, the whiffed edit, the open sprint)
2. What was the EARLIEST mistake? (the decision 20–40s earlier that made the death likely)
3. Did I choose this fight, or did it choose me?
4. What did I know about mats / HP / zone / third parties — and what did I ignore?
5. What was the better play, in one sentence?
6. Have I died this exact way before?

Question 2 is the one that separates useful review from clip-watching. The elimination at 8:43 was usually decided at 8:20 — a late rotation, a fight taken from low ground, looting past the zone timer. Fixing last-mistakes makes you marginally better; fixing earliest-mistakes changes your results.

Question 6 is where improvement actually lives. One bad peek is noise. The same bad peek four times in a week is a pattern — and patterns are fixable, because you can practice against them and check next week whether the count dropped. Keep a running tally per mistake type (a notes app is fine: "no-wall peek: ×4 this week"). If you want names for the patterns, our guide to the seven most common causes of death is a ready-made taxonomy.

How long does this take?

Done honestly: 10–15 minutes per session if you die 6–10 times and spend about a minute per death, plus the tally. That's the theory.

Why almost everyone quits VOD review

In practice, most players who start VOD reviewing stop within a couple of weeks, for predictable reasons:

  • The friction stack: remember to save the clip, find the file, scrub to the death, stay honest, write it down — after every single death, forever.
  • It's emotionally backwards: the best time to review is right after a tilting death, which is exactly when nobody wants to rewatch it.
  • No memory: without a tally, this week's review can't see last month's pattern, so every session starts from zero.
  • Self-diagnosis is hard: you tend to blame the last mistake (or the opponent's "cracked aim") rather than the earliest mistake, because that's the comfortable answer.

None of this makes VOD review less valuable — it makes it valuable and rarely done, like flossing. If you can sustain the manual habit, do it; the checklist above is the whole method.
